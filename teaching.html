Teaching Philosophy

Goals & Methods

In manuscripts that I have been translating, Leibniz wrote that “improving the intellect,” rather than obtaining truths, is actually “the most powerful use of mathematical demonstrations.” Most students of mathematics go on to careers and further study in other fields, where the value of their education is realized as an ability to sustain clear and careful reasoning. I see myself as a coach for students strengthening their ability to think. If I can provide opportunities to practice articulating precise, logical reasoning, I will have achieved a measure of success. One of my calculus students wrote that my course “changed [their] perspective of mathematics to a system of logical thinking and problem solving rather than only a computational tool.”

My students spend a long time with a few important arguments that make up the heart of each course. In Calculus III, they learn to derive Kepler’s law that planets follow elliptical orbits; I help them extract and remember a skeleton of the proof, and teach them to recover the details on the fly, putting to use the cross product rules and seeing the power of these rules in context. In probability, I want every student to understand why a binomial distribution looks Gaussian in one regime and Poisson in another. In applied linear algebra, my course is arranged around the singular value decomposition.

I write my own weekly “packets” that blend exercises with complete lecture notes. I feel that my students should receive something of value from me, something uniquely my own to offer. I have written full packets for six courses. One student called them “concise, straight to the point, and still in depth.” Another wrote, “I came out of this class feeling like I could ‘read’ math, and [this] prepared me to take some higher level math classes.”

After studying their packet, students prepare to explain the exercises aloud in short presentations given in class on a daily or weekly basis, followed by Q&A. I am motivated by the benefits students report: “It’s one thing to work on a problem and understand it independently but it requires another layer of understanding to explain … it to the class.” I have found that presentations let the class see a diversity of approaches that their peers bring to a problem, let me observe learning progress and sticking points, and reveal any growing reliance on learning crutches.

I seek to empower students to overcome math anxiety by centering the artistic and contemplative aspects of mathematical practice, rather than the performative and competitive ones. Though I am pleased to see good exam scores, I reward a student’s reflection and creativity with excited engagement. A Calculus III student reported that they “appreciated how the instructor wanted students to focus on learning instead of worrying about grades.”

Belonging

In my experience, belonging is less an attitude than a concrete matter of students’ expectations for a course and their feeling that they can manage it. In order to challenge the top students while including those who are new to the subject, each assessment should include simple “knowledge” questions to allow beginners to demonstrate learning, and should progress to just one or two final challenge questions that separate the top students. In class I make a point of welcoming simple questions. Rather than answering tersely so as not to bore the others, I adopt the language and level of the student in an answer that validates the question, always looking for something interesting at every level.

Digital accessibility is a serious challenge for math instructors striving for inclusive practices. Through my efforts to develop course websites, I found a method of providing accessible (screen-reader-friendly) MathML embedded in HTML. I guided a CS major who implemented this solution and then wrote a tutorial that we have made public.
